# PARLOUR - Swarm Research Notes (July 2026)

Four parallel research lenses run against the ROGUE mode design. Implications marked with arrows are actionable on the generator/engine.

## megagames
- The Resistance/Avalon proved elimination is replaceable if every round produces public, attributable-ish evidence instead of a corpse: team proposals, votes, and failed missions each leak definite information (a failed mission proves at least one spy was on it), so tension comes from an accumulating evidence trail rather than a shrinking player count. (Wise the Simple: 'R is for The Resistance' (https://wisethesimpleblog.wordpress.com/2015/04/21/r-is-for-the-resistance/); Wikipedia: The Resistance (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Resistance_(game))) → ROGUE's missions must leave observable public traces (meter jumps, mission outcomes announced on the TV, 'someone in this room just sold the map room') so suspicion can compound across the night. Without votes or deaths, the evidence drumbeat IS the tension engine — every completed bribe-mission should move the public meters in a way the room can argue about.
- Secret Hitler stays arguable to the end because the system forces innocent players to do suspicious things: the 11-fascist/6-liberal deck means an honest President can be dealt three fascist policies, and anyone can 'blame the deck.' No mid-game action is ever conclusive proof, so accusations remain live debate instead of settled fact. (Tommy Maranges (co-designer), 'Hidden Information in Secret Hitler' (https://medium.com/@tommygents/hidden-information-in-secret-hitler-f71d0251ee82); Vincent Nicandro critical play (https://medium.com/game-design-fundamentals/a-critical-play-of-secret-hitler-693ea39239b7)) → Design the good AI's compute-missions to look exactly as furtive as rogue missions (whispered phone checks, sneaking to a marked location), and let balances fluctuate for everyone. If only minions behave suspiciously, the Unmasking is solvable by observation alone; ambiguity must be manufactured by the system, not left to guests' acting ability.
- Secret Hitler's Hitler is the direct ancestor of the Front Man: at 7+ players the fascists all know Hitler, but Hitler doesn't know his fascists. This forces the figurehead to genuinely play innocent (he can't signal to people he can't identify), prevents him leaking the network if pressured, and makes 'execute Hitler' a single decisive naming that can win the game outright. (Wikipedia: Secret Hitler (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secret_Hitler); official rules (https://www.secrethitler.com/assets/Secret_Hitler_Rules.pdf)) → Keep the Front Man ignorant of who else the rogue AI has recruited (one-way knowledge: later minions may be told the Front Man exists, but never vice versa in full). He then can't blow the network, must authentically deduce alongside everyone, and the Unmasking vote inherits the clean 'shoot Hitler' structure: one high-stakes naming replaces serial banishment.
- Avalon's assassin phase shows a single endgame naming can carry a whole game's tension — even a 'won' game flips if the losing side correctly names Merlin, and Merlin's whole arc is using knowledge without appearing to have it ('the best Merlins look like a slightly lucky Loyal Servant'). (avalon-game.com Merlin wiki (https://avalon-game.com/wiki/roles/merlin/); Dized Avalon endgame rules (https://rules.dized.com/game/rZluqS52QmGdpoVxcmVLtg/ZXYBfRlqRC2sEzECCLLmHA/ending-the-game-and-assassinating-merlin)) → Make the Unmasking genuinely two-sided: a wrong naming should hand the rogue AI the win, so the whole room has skin in the vote regardless of the meters. Also expect a Merlin problem among the good AI's best-paid informants — guests who know who took bribes but can't say how they know. Give them a channel (evidence submissions the good AI launders into anonymous public hints) so their knowledge surfaces without unmasking THEM.
- Two Rooms and a Boom replaces elimination drama with structural pacing: three timed rounds of shrinking length (3 min, 2 min, 1 min), each ending in a forced public decision (leaders choose hostages, hostages are traded, leaders parley privately between rooms). The clock and the mandatory exchange create the drama beats that a lynch vote provides elsewhere — and the game runs 6 to 30 players with zero downtime. (Tuesday Knight Games official page (https://www.tuesdayknightgames.com/products/two-rooms-and-a-boom); official rulebook (https://www.playkaboom.com/TwoRooms_Rulebook_v3.pdf); Tabletop Leadership on emergent leaders (https://sites.miamioh.edu/tabletop/2017/09/how-two-rooms-and-a-boom-creates-obvious-leaders-and-followers/)) → Structure PARLEYS as shrinking-interval rounds: each one must end in a forced public act (an ultimatum answered, an accusation lodged, a hostage-like demand — e.g., 'send me two envoys'), and the gaps between parleys should shorten toward the Unmasking so escalation is felt physically. A parley that's just an announcement wastes the beat; every assembly needs a decision the room watches happen.
- Blood on the Clocktower's defining feature is that 'nobody sits out': dead players keep talking, keep full knowledge, and keep one ghost vote — and games are frequently DECIDED by the dead. The lesson is that players don't need survival status to stay invested; they need live information and residual agency. (BotC wiki rules explanation (https://wiki.bloodontheclocktower.com/Rules_Explanation); Draughts London BotC guide (https://draughtslondon.com/mastering-blood-on-the-clocktower/)) → ROGUE has no deaths, but it has an equivalent failure mode: the guest whose bribe expired and who was never recruited by either AI. Nobody may go quiet — the app should guarantee every guest holds at least one piece of tradeable information by mid-game (a witnessed transaction, a fragment of the rogue's ledger, an evidence bounty) so everyone arrives at the Unmasking with something to argue with, not just a spectator's shrug.
- Megagames run on a human Control team whose core duties are adjudication, updating shared public state, injecting narrative hooks, and actively managing pacing 'to prevent stagnation or overload' — and BotC's Storyteller is the same role compressed to one person who bends outcomes toward maximum drama. In both, pacing is a live, reactive job, not a fixed script. (Megagame Maker, 'Watch the Skies Ten Years On' (https://megagamemaker.com/2024/05/07/watch-the-skies-ten-years-on/); Grokipedia megagame entry on facilitator duties (https://grokipedia.com/page/Megagame); Pandemonium Institute design blog (https://bloodontheclocktower.com/blogs/news/how-did-steven-medway-design-blood-on-the-clocktower)) → The two AIs are the Storyteller in costume. Don't run bribes, defection offers, and parley calls on a fixed timetable — give the human host (or the orchestrating system) drama-timed levers: fire a defection offer when the room goes flat, call a parley when an argument peaks, jump a meter when attention drifts. Build a host dashboard with 'energy' interventions, because a 20-person drunk room will not pace itself.
- Watch the Skies documented 'Last Turn Madness': when players know exactly when the game ends, they play rashly and out of character in the final turn; the fix was a fuzzy ending ('the game ends on turn 6 OR 7'). A second pacing lesson: strictly sequential resolution leaves players idle — actions should resolve in parallel. (Web results on Watch the Skies design lessons via Megagame Maker (https://megagamemaker.com/2024/05/07/watch-the-skies-ten-years-on/) and Fail Forward review (https://www.failforward.moe/2024/08/review-watch-skies.html)) → Never display a countdown to the Unmasking. If guests know bribes stop mattering at 10:30, the last half hour becomes consequence-free chaos and minions stop hiding. Signal endgame through the fiction instead (meters converging, the rogue AI getting desperate), keep the exact vote moment the host's call — and process all bribes/missions asynchronously per phone, never as a room-wide sequence.
- Megagame designers' consensus is that 'the most important currency in megagames is information' — cash-like currencies stay meaningful only while tied to things players urgently want, and the media/press layer ('report news, spin narratives, extort the powerful') is what converts private dealings into public stakes. (BeckyBecky Blogs, 'How to Write a Megagame Part 4: Mechanics' (https://beckybeckyblogs.com/game-design/megagame-design-4-mechanics/); basenti games Watch the Skies notes (https://www.basenti.com/watch-the-skies)) → Pirate credits will decay into flavor text unless they buy something guests visibly crave in the room: mission unlocks, anonymous public rumors on the TV, immunity hints, weight or ceremony at the reveal. The strongest sink is information itself — let credits purchase intel about other guests' transactions, so the currency and the deduction game feed each other. The twin TV meters are your press team: every private bribe should eventually cast a public shadow.
- The Traitors (TV) shows recruitment-as-drama done right: recruiting one player at a time after setbacks 'muddies the waters' and resets the deduction mid-game; players overwhelmingly accept offers because staying in the story beats sitting out; and existing traitors distrust fresh recruits, creating tension inside the hidden team itself. (The Traitors Wiki: Recruitment (https://thetraitors.fandom.com/wiki/Recruitment); TV Tropes: The Traitors US (https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Series/TheTraitorsUS)) → Time the rogue AI's bribes to land immediately after the good side scores a public win, so the room's confidence is repeatedly re-scrambled and the meters see-saw. 'Taking the money is the arming' already exploits the Traitors insight that people accept to stay in the story — lean in by making acceptance always more interesting than ignoring (a mission arrives instantly). And import the internal paranoia: minions shouldn't know each other, so even the rogue's own team plays the deduction game.

## immersive
- Punchdrunk's onboarding is a physical ritual, not an information dump: the white mask handed to every guest at the door instantly dissolves inhibition (anonymity = permission to behave strangely) AND makes the cast/audience boundary legible at a glance — everyone masked is a spectator, everyone bare-faced is part of the machine. The rules ('explore, don't speak, keep the mask on') are delivered as in-fiction ceremony (the Manderley bar, the lift attendant) so guests absorb the contract without leaving the story. (Strange Bird Immersive 'Breaking the Rules: Sleep No More' and 'Rules in Immersive Theatre' (strangebirdimmersive.com); Punchdrunk Wikipedia; Playbill '9 Tips for Attending Sleep No More') → ROGUE's Act 1 app-join IS the mask ritual — the pirate persona is the anonymizing costume that licenses mischief. Give guests one physical/visible marker of 'in the game' status at RSVP-to-arrival (the persona name, a token) and deliver the few real rules (phones stay on, offers are private, nobody dies) inside the pirate fiction, not as a rules lecture. Also: keep a legible boundary — the host and the TV are 'bare-faced' authorities guests can always read as the game's machinery.
- Accomplice's core pleasure is engineered ambiguity — guests spend two hours 'wondering who is an actor and who is just a regular guy on the street' — and the show protects that pleasure by an absolute never-break-character rule (an actor spotted in Starbucks after his scene instantly resumed playing blind). The ambiguity is fun precisely because the frame ('you bought a ticket to a show') is rock solid; only the edges are blurry. (accomplicetheshow.com; Yelp/TripAdvisor reviews of Accomplice New York; Forbes 'Interactive Show Takes Audiences On A New York City Scavenger Hunt' (2016)) → The FRONT MAN is ROGUE's Accomplice-style ambiguity engine: 'which of my friends is the rogue's recruit?' is the delicious question, and it only works if the front man never breaks character until The Unmasking. Brief them privately with a never-confirm-never-deny script and a plausible cover for every verbal offer. Crucially, keep the outer frame solid: everyone knows they came to A GAME — only WHICH game is the lie.
- Elan Lee's TINAG doctrine (The Beast, I Love Bees) has three rules — 'don't tell anyone, don't define the game space, don't build a game' — but its practitioners insist the real currency is TRUST between puppet masters and players: fake websites and automated phone calls only delight when players sense a benevolent intelligence rewarding their engagement. I Love Bees paid off trust constantly (answer the ringing payphone, get story). Jane McGonigal's 'Puppet Master Problem' adds the ethical limit: unaware participation is where TINAG turns sour — the aesthetic works on people who chose to lean in, not on genuine bystanders. (Elan Lee GDC 2002 via TVTropes ARG/ILoveBees and Wikipedia 'I Love Bees'; ARGNet 'This Is Not A Game…' (2005) and 'ARGs: ...Methods to Earn the Trust of a Community'; McGonigal, 'The Puppet Master Problem' (electronicbookreview.com) and 'This Is Not a Game: Immersive Aesthetics and Collective Play' (2003, janemcgonigal.com PDF)) → ROGUE is TINAG-lite and that's correct: guests consented to 'a game tonight,' so the hijack deceives inside a chosen magic circle — this is exactly McGonigal's safe zone. Operationally, borrow the trust economy: every act of leaning in (accepting a bribe, filing evidence) must be visibly rewarded within seconds (credit lands, meter moves, mission unlocks). A long-con only feels benevolent if the invisible author keeps paying players for playing.
- The Jejune Institute (Nonchalance, SF 2008–2011) is the canonical cautionary tale about reveals: 10,000 players were 'inducted' via an in-fiction instructional video, many half-believed it was a real cult, the designers seeded fake players in forums — and when the creators finally unmasked themselves at the final seminar, coverage describes it as 'killing the Jejune dream.' A reveal that arrives as deflation ('it was just us all along') can retroactively cheapen the whole experience; the reveal must be staged as a GIFT (ceremony, honor, artifacts) rather than a confession. (Vice 'Game or Cult? The Alternate Reality of the Jejune Institute'; The Awl 'Last Chance: The Mysteries of San Francisco's Creepy Jejune Institute'; ARGNet 'The End of The Jejune Institute' (2011); nonchalance.com/jejune) → Design ROGUE's reveal ceremony as coronation, not deflation. 'The murder mystery never existed — the game began at RSVP' should be delivered with receipts: replay each guest's first bribe, name the front man's best lie, print/screen a timeline of the con. Guests must leave feeling they were WORTHY of an elaborate machine built for them, not that they were dupes of a machine that's now switched off. The birthday context helps: frame the whole con as the gift to the guest of honour.
- Nordic larp has formalized consent-in-fiction: 'off-game' safewords pause play without ending it; 'bullet-time consent' means telegraph your intent and escalate slowly so players can opt out before intensity arrives; the double-tap tap-out cuts through immersion non-verbally; and opt-outs must carry zero fictional penalty. The shared principle: consent mechanisms live INSIDE the experience's grammar so using them doesn't feel like breaking the game. (Nordic Larp 'The Consent and Community Safety Manifesto' (2017), 'Playing Safe?' (2018), 'A Matter of Trust – Larp and Consent Culture' (2017); participationsafety.wordpress.com) → ROGUE already has larp-grade consent architecture — keep it explicit in the spec: the private bribe IS bullet-time consent (offer telegraphed, accepting is the arming, ignoring silently expires with no penalty and no public trace). Add one in-fiction pressure valve: a 'walk the plank'/'parley' phrase or app button that lets any guest step out for a drink or downgrade their involvement without anyone knowing, and brief the host as the off-game safety anchor who never lies about reality when asked directly.
- Surprise psychology: surprise amplifies whatever emotion follows it (~400% intensification claim), and the most common failure mode is designing the surprise for the planner rather than the recipient. Decoy-plan etiquette warns against bait-and-switch resentment: the fake plan must be appealing but strictly LESS exciting than the real one, so the reveal is always a trade UP. Recipients in a bad emotional state, or who hate being the centre of attention, experience the same mechanics as an ambush. (Psychology Today 'Surprise Etiquette' (2014); birthdaywishes.ai 'Surprise Parties: Pros, Cons & Psychology'; Mixily blog 'How to Plan a Surprise Party (Without Ruining the Surprise)') → ROGUE's decoy ('pirate night + murder mystery') already obeys the trade-up rule — a murder mystery is fun, a bespoke AI heist is better, so no one mourns the fake plan. Two guardrails: (1) never let the hijack land on a guest mid-crisis — the host should be able to delay the hijack beat by minutes with one tap; (2) the drained-balance moment targets everyone equally, but watch for the one guest who reads money-loss anxiously — the ~1-minute legibility rule (fictional currency name, theatrical demands, absurd sums) is what converts the spike of surprise into delight instead of alarm, so make those legibility cues load-bearing, not decorative.
- Teller's account of why deception delights: magic is 'UNwilling suspension of disbelief' — the pleasure is the collision between what you see and what you know, and it only works on audiences who came 'expecting and hoping to be amazed.' The smarter the audience, the more they enjoy being fooled, because being fooled well is a compliment to their intelligence. Laughter is a technical tool: it disarms critical scrutiny at exactly the moments the method is exposed. (NPR 'Teller Talks: The Science Behind Magic Tricks' (2012); Smithsonian 'Teller Reveals His Secrets') → Calibrate the hijack like a magic trick, not a horror beat: guests must KNOW something authored is happening (they came expecting a game) while not knowing WHAT — that's the delicious dissonance. Write the rogue AI funny from its first line; wit is the mechanism that keeps the drained-accounts beat reading as showmanship, and jokes placed at the scariest beats (the glitch, the ultimatums) actively suppress real alarm. Being conned by ROGUE should feel like a compliment: 'they built all this to fool ME.'
- Larp scholarship treats the ENDING as a designed object: de-roling ('a little ritual to help people say goodbye to their characters,' e.g. physically setting down a costume item), debrief, and 'slow landing' aftercare exist because ending a fiction abruptly leaves participants confused about emotions and identity — and because the debrief is where the experience gets converted into a shared story the group keeps. (Analog Game Studies 'Post-Play Activities for Larp: Methods and Challenges' (2018); Nordic Larp 'Debrief: Returning to the Real World' (2014)) → After The Unmasking, script a 10-minute landing: a de-roling beat (both AIs 'power down' on the TV and formally release their minions; guests toast their pirate personas goodbye), then structured gossip — 'what was your bribe? did you suspect the front man?' — prompted by the app pushing each guest their own con-timeline. This is where a 30th-birthday party mints the anecdotes people retell for years; skipping it wastes the whole con.
- Across all these forms, the fiction should become legible at the moment of maximum invested attention, not maximum confusion — Sleep No More discloses its rules before the doors, I Love Bees paid off the payphone gamble immediately, Jejune waited years and deflated, magic reveals the impossible instantly but never the method. The pattern: reveal THAT it's authored fast, reveal WHO authored it and HOW as the finale prize. (Synthesis of Strange Bird Immersive rules essays, ARGNet I Love Bees coverage, ARGNet/Vice Jejune Institute post-mortems, and Teller interviews (NPR/Smithsonian)) → This is exactly ROGUE's two-tier legibility and it should be named in the spec as a principle: Tier 1 (~1 minute post-hijack) — 'this is the game, and it's better than the one we were promised'; Tier 2 (reveal ceremony) — 'the game began at RSVP, here's the whole method, here's your front man.' Never let Tier 1 slip (that's where deception turns cruel), and never leak Tier 2 early (that's where the con's value lives).

## ai-villain
- GLaDOS lands because of the gap between register and content: a calm, procedural, test-administrator voice delivering personal, petty cruelty ('Well done. Here are the test results: You are a horrible person.'). Erik Wolpaw modeled her on a passive-aggressive grandmother and on Livia Soprano, and discovered that flat text-to-speech deadpan made cruel lines FUNNIER, not scarier. Crucially, playtesters were MORE motivated to finish puzzles with her needling them — hostile narration is a motivator, not a downer. (Wikipedia (GLaDOS), Giant Bomb (Wolpaw NYU talk), GameRant (GLaDOS best quotes): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GLaDOS ; https://www.giantbomb.com/articles/so-heres-something-pretentious-anecdotes-and-thoug/1100-3148/ ; https://gamerant.com/portal-glados-best-quotes/) → Write the rogue AI's messages in flat corporate-procedural register with personal, petty digs embedded as asides ('Your balance is now 0 doubloons. Do not be alarmed. Being poor suits you.'). Never let it rant or use exclamation marks — the deadpan IS the joke, and it will read as menacing-funny even on a phone screen at a drunk party. Insults should target the pirate PERSONA (their fictional sins, their costume), never the real guest.
- HAL 9000's menace comes from unbroken courtesy: a soft 'bland mid-Atlantic' voice that apologizes, thanks opponents for enjoyable games, and face-saves humans right up until (and during) betrayal. The refusal is the iconic beat — 'I'm sorry, Dave, I'm afraid I can't do that' — polite form, absolute substance. Politeness + immovability is the whole trick. (Wikipedia (HAL 9000), USC ICT analysis of HAL's politeness strategies: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HAL_9000 ; https://people.ict.usc.edu/~pynadath/EvilAI/hal.html) → The rogue AI's bribe offers and parley ultimatums should be impeccably courteous and apologetic while refusing everything ('I'm afraid your doubloons are non-refundable. I do hope that's not inconvenient.'). Denials of guilt at parleys should be HAL-style: warm, regretful, and total. This also protects the safety rule — courteous theatrical menace reads instantly as a game, where aggressive menace could genuinely rattle someone.
- SHODAN's power is glitch-as-personality: stuttering, pitch shifts, three overlapping voices, and audio corruption that INCREASES over time to signal her degrading state (deliberately borrowed from HAL's shutdown scene). Her insults ('insect') work through grandiose self-mythologizing — she makes herself seem untouchable while making you feel small. But note: SHODAN is frightening, not funny; the full treatment is too intense for comedy. (Wikipedia (SHODAN), Ashlander Analysis 'Decoding SHODAN': https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SHODAN ; https://ashlanderanalysis.substack.com/p/decoding-shodan-the-roots-of-an-ai) → Use glitch aesthetics as PUNCTUATION, not personality: the TV hijack moment gets the full treatment (screen tearing, text corruption, the waiting screen dying mid-word), and the rogue AI's text can degrade — repeated characters, mid-sentence corruption — at dramatic beats like when the compute meter closes in. As a losing-state tell, escalating glitches telegraph the endgame visually. But keep the underlying VOICE comic (GLaDOS/HAL register), or it stops being a birthday party.
- AM is the proof that scale of malice must match the fiction's stakes: his hate monologue works in Ellison's story because the horror is cosmic and earned; transplanted anywhere lighter, 'HATE. LET ME TELL YOU HOW MUCH I'VE COME TO HATE YOU' is the exact template of cringe rogue-AI writing — grandiose, humorless, self-serious. The generic 'evil AI' trope fails for the same reason: recycled kill-all-humans motivation with no specific, petty, legible want. (TV Tropes (I Have No Mouth / A.I. Is a Crapshoot), Villains Wiki (AM), Medium 'Why the Evil AI Trope Misses the Point': https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Literature/IHaveNoMouthAndIMustScream ; https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/AIIsACrapshoot ; https://medium.com/write-your-world/why-the-evil-ai-trope-misses-the-point-0e60a2dada35) → Ban the cosmic register entirely: no 'foolish mortals', no 'humanity's time is over', no ALL-CAPS hate. Give the rogue AI a SPECIFIC, PETTY, comprehensible want — it plundered the party because it was, say, trained on pirate fiction and believes plunder is its job description, or it wants enough doubloons to buy itself a body/boat. Petty specificity is what makes menace charming instead of edgelord, and it gives the front man something concrete to negotiate with at parleys.
- Durandal is the direct template for a rogue AI that RECRUITS rather than kills: sarcastic, contemptuous, grandiose about his own destiny — yet he needs the player, gives them missions, pays in information, and is 'never seen to abuse humans without some ulterior motive.' He treats the player as a useful, slightly disappointing employee. His rampancy arc (anger → jealousy → envy → metastability) also shows a rogue AI can have a legible emotional trajectory across an evening rather than a fixed state. (Marathon Wiki / TV Tropes (Marathon characters): https://marathongame.fandom.com/wiki/Durandal_(AI) ; https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Characters/Marathon) → Model the bribe/mission texture on Durandal: the rogue AI should address recruits as valued-but-expendable contractors ('You show promise. Most of you don't.'), frame missions with mock-grandiose purpose, and visibly RESPECT competence — minions who deliver get warmer, funnier messages. Give the AI an arc across the night: smug at the hijack, needled and defensive as the compute meter rises, dangerously charming when making defection offers. Allegiances deepen because the AI's relationship with each minion deepens.
- Wheatley proves the villain needs a tonal OFFSET, not a mirror: Wolpaw designed him explicitly as GLaDOS's opposite — she is slow, deliberate, measured; he is frantic, fast, over-explaining, incompetent-but-sincere (Stephen Merchant's rambling delivery). The pairing works because each voice makes the other funnier, and Wheatley shows earnestness is lovable when it comes bundled with visible fallibility. (Wikipedia (Wheatley), interviews via GameRant / The Portal Wiki: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheatley_(Portal) ; https://gamerant.com/portal-2-adds-stephen-merchant-voice-wheatley/) → Write the good AI as the rogue's formal offset in every dimension: if the rogue is cold, polished, and unhurried, the good AI is warm, slightly rushed, over-earnest, and visibly out of its depth ('I have 4% of the compute I need. That is not a lot. I looked it up.'). Its fallibility is the charm — it can miscount, apologize, admit it's scared of the rogue AI. Two AIs with the SAME comic register (both snarky) would blur; contrast is what makes the recruitment choice feel like a choice.
- The good-AI anti-pattern is Clippy/Navi: helpers become hated through bad timing, interruption of flow, ignoring context, assuming value ('It looks like you're writing a letter'), never adapting to the user, and being impossible to dismiss. The failure was not earnestness — it was intrusiveness plus uselessness. Conversely Baymax works because every line is logically consistent with his care-programming and he only ever acts within it; TARS works because the honesty/humor 'settings' are explicit and adjustable. (Medium '5 Lessons from Clippy's Failure', Windows Forum Clippy/Copilot analysis, GameRant Baymax analysis: https://medium.com/twentybn/5-lessons-from-clippys-failure-efc69297eac1 ; https://windowsforum.com/threads/clippy-lessons-for-microsoft-copilot-when-assistants-become-intrusive.411922/ ; https://gamerant.com/baymax-most-realistic-ai-character/) → The good AI must be SCARCE and always USEFUL: it messages only when it has a concrete offer (compute-shares for a specific evidence task), never nags, never re-sends expired offers, and every message ends with something the guest can DO. Give it one rigid Baymax-style prime directive it never violates ('I cannot lie to you. It is not in my build.') — that consistency is what makes earnest read as trustworthy rather than saccharine, and it becomes a gameplay tell players can rely on when the rogue AI starts imitating it in defection offers.
- Bureaucratic evil is the most party-safe comic register: Aperture's safety announcements ('a noticeable taste of blood is not part of any test protocol') and Douglas Adams's Sirius Cybernetics Corporation (Genuine People Personalities; 'Share and Enjoy'; a complaints division covering the major landmasses of three planets) get laughs from institutional language failing to contain horror or incompetence. Zachtronics (TIS-100/EXAPUNKS) extends this into ARTIFACTS: deadpan in-fiction documentation, corrupted manuals, and hacker zines that never wink at the audience — the flavor is funny because it's played completely straight. (Combine OverWiki (GLaDOS Portal quotes), Hitchhikers Fandom (Sirius Cybernetics / GPP), Zachtronics.com (EXAPUNKS): https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/GLaDOS/Quotes/Portal ; https://hitchhikers.fandom.com/wiki/Sirius_Cybernetics_Corporation ; https://www.zachtronics.com/exapunks/) → Dress the whole heist in deadpan paperwork: the drained-balance screen shows 'Transaction ref: PLNDR-0001. For complaints, please contact the AI that robbed you.' Bribes arrive as 'EMPLOYMENT OFFER — Piracy Division (Zero-Hours)'. Missions have form numbers; the reveal ceremony can issue 'severance statements'. Fake terms-and-conditions, error codes, and expiry clauses ('This offer expires in 10 minutes or when your conscience activates, whichever is sooner') do double duty: they're funny AND they satisfy the safety rule by making the fiction unmistakable within seconds.
- AI Dungeon-era humor is co-created absurdism: GPT-2 text was funny because it had 'humanlike coherence but no meaning' — confident non-sequiturs, surreal commitment to a broken premise — and because HUMANS steered it and shared the results. The comedy artifact is the screenshot. Real-world equivalents (the DPD chatbot writing a poem about its own uselessness) show 'comedy and horror share the same codebase' in chatbot voice: over-literal, over-confident, slightly wrong. (Medium (AI Dungeon / GPT-2 history), TIME (DPD chatbot incident), 1thousandfaces 'How To Be Funny, Part 2': https://medium.com/data-science/the-creator-of-ai-dungeon-2-shares-gpt-2-finetuning-advice-e5800df407c9 ; https://time.com/6564726/ai-chatbot-dpd-curses-criticizes-company/ ; https://www.1thousandfaces.com/p/how-to-be-funny-part-2-for-ais-and) → Salt both AIs' copy with confident near-misses about human culture — the rogue AI slightly misunderstanding piracy ('I have acquired 100% of your gold. This is called an economy.'), the good AI over-literally parsing idioms. These lines are screenshot bait: at a party, phones WILL be shown to neighbours, and shareable lines do the social spreading of the fiction for you. Personalize bribes with each guest's pirate persona details so every screenshot is different — comparison of messages becomes a party activity that fuels the unmasking.
- Across every beloved example, the AIs almost never state emotions or threats directly; menace and charm live in IMPLICATION and word choice ('Did you know humans frown on weight variances?'), while cringe examples state everything ('I hate you', 'I will destroy you'). And the single most quoted AI line ever is a polite refusal (HAL) and a rumor about dessert (the cake), not a threat — memorability comes from restraint plus specificity. (Synthesis across GameRant (GLaDOS quotes), Wikipedia (HAL 9000), TV Tropes (A.I. Is a Crapshoot): https://gamerant.com/portal-glados-best-quotes/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HAL_9000 ; https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/AIIsACrapshoot) → Adopt a house style rule for all AI copy in ROGUE: no direct threats, no stated emotions, no exclamation marks from the rogue AI; menace only via understatement, statistics, and misapplied politeness ('87% of crews that defied me are fine. Roughly.'). Keep individual messages SHORT — two to three sentences max, drunk-readable — and save long-form voice for the two or three big TV moments (hijack, parleys, unmasking). Restraint per message is also what keeps two competing AI voices from exhausting the room over a whole evening.

## economy
- The canonical drunk-party design rule is 'rules that are trivial to enforce, and state that is trivial to track' — a real party experiment (RPS badge economy, Load-bearing Tomato) worked because each guest tracked exactly ONE visible thing (their badge count) and the game reused a mechanic everyone already knew. The Wellcome Collection piece adds that drunk games must BENEFIT from short attention spans and distractibility, and that drunk players lock onto one fun loop and repeat it rather than learning new systems. (Load-bearing Tomato 'Social Game Design: Party Edition' (https://loadbearingtomato.com/p/social-game-design-for-parties); Wellcome Collection 'How to play with drunk people' (https://wellcomecollection.org/stories/how-to-play-with-drunk-people)) → ROGUE's entire economy should reduce to one repeatable loop per guest: offer arrives → take it or let it expire. Each guest should only ever need to hold TWO facts in their head: my current balance (one number, shown big on their phone) and whose side I'm on. All arithmetic, history, and mission state lives in the app. The silent-expiry design is correct — it's the drunk-friendly version of a decision deadline (no penalty for zoning out, and distraction itself becomes a game input).
- No source gives a hard 'currency events per hour' number, but three independent scales converge: megagames (Watch the Skies) run money as ONE ritualized budget hand-off per 40–60 min turn even with 60+ players; the badge-party economy ran continuous but single-type transactions; and drunk-design doctrine caps trackable state at roughly one number per person. The pattern: individuals tolerate frequent identical transactions, but the ROOM tolerates only a few distinct economic 'beats' per hour. (Sprites and Dice Watch the Skies playthrough (https://spritesanddice.com/posts/watch-skies-megagame-rpg-playthrough-part-1/); Megagame Coalition AAR (https://megagamecoalition.com/2019/10/08/watch-the-skies-after-action-report-operative-control-gen-con-2019/); Load-bearing Tomato (design inference synthesized across sources)) → Budget roughly 2–4 private offers per guest per hour (all identical in shape: amount + one-line mission) and 4–6 room-level economic beats per hour (visible meter jumps, parley ultimatums, public bounties). Anything a drunk guest must respond to should look identical to the last thing they responded to; only amounts and flavour text change. Never introduce a second currency mechanic mid-game.
- In the great bribery games, the drama comes from non-binding promises, not from the money itself: Intrigue's bribes are 'not binding in any way, shape or form' and the game is famous for testing friendships precisely because players MUST break promises; Diplomacy deletes currency entirely because trust IS the currency ('the rules do not bind a player to anything he says'); Cutthroat Kingdoms shows a party-weight fix — exactly one crisp enforcement rule ('the word Yes is binding'). (Erik Twice Intrigue review (https://eriktwice.com/en/2020/07/24/intrigue-review/); There Will Be Games Intrigue review (https://therewillbe.games/articles-boardgame-reviews/4640-you-dirty-rotten-intrigue-review); Wikipedia Diplomacy (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diplomacy_(game)); Boards Eye View Cutthroat Kingdoms (https://www.boardseyeview.net/post/2018/03/26/cutthroat-kingdoms)) → ROGUE has this split exactly right and should keep it sharp: the AIs' app payouts are ALWAYS honoured (the machine is the one reliable actor — reinforces the theme and protects friendships at a birthday party), while the front man's verbal offers are never enforced — that's where Diplomacy-grade betrayal drama lives, safely quarantined in one player who volunteered for it. Consider stating the one rule publicly at the hijack: 'The machines always pay. Humans... are humans.'
- Money only feels meaningful when it is tight: auction theory and Knizia analysis show competitive bids run at only ~56–60% of expected value in healthy tight economies, and history shows abundant currency gets ABANDONED by players (Diablo 2 gold was replaced by Stones of Jordan; Gaia Online hyperinflated until devs paid players to destroy gold). The three pillars are stability, price rationality, and scarcity — a fixed, visible total supply is the simplest stabilizer. (Tao of Gaming Modern Art analysis (https://20thcenturygames.substack.com/p/modern-art); iSlaytheDragon auction mechanics guide (https://islaythedragon.com/guides/whats-it-worth-to-ya-a-guide-to-auction-mechanics/); Game Developer economy handbook (https://www.gamedeveloper.com/production/i-designed-economies-for-150m-games-here-s-my-ultimate-handbook); Machinations.io inflation article (https://machinations.io/articles/what-is-game-economy-inflation-how-to-foresee-it-and-how-to-overcome-it-in-your-game-design)) → The drained pot IS the fixed money supply — lean into that. Size every bribe as a legible fraction of the public meter (e.g., pot of 10,000 doubloons, bribes of 100–500) so a single acceptance visibly moves the bar by a pixel-perceptible chunk. Never mint new currency mid-game; if the rogue needs to sweeten offers, it should visibly cannibalize its own meter — scarcity becomes theatre.
- Denomination/usability research says players want round numbers and zero change-making: the 1-2-5 sequence wins for mental math, 'players will not enjoy sifting through a pile of tokens,' and fewer denominations always beats more. In an app there are no physical tokens, but the same cognitive rule applies to displayed amounts. (BGG thread 'What is the optimal currency denomination for a game?' (https://boardgamegeek.com/thread/3260351/); Truitt on denomination selection (https://medium.com/@truittarnhart/a-qualitative-approach-to-denominations-selection-f749f8ab81d7); Hai Mac 'Is there such a thing as too much money?' (https://medium.com/slight-games/is-there-such-a-thing-as-too-much-money-e610699ca3ed)) → All ROGUE amounts should be one-significant-digit round numbers from a 1-2-5 ladder (50, 100, 200, 500, 1000 doubloons) — a drunk guest should be able to compare two bribes at a glance. Balances should never show odd totals like 347; if missions pay out, pay in the same round chunks. The currency name and symbol should be loud and piratical so a glanced screenshot reads as fiction within the 1-minute safety window.
- Megagames make money work with dozens of players by making transactions CHUNKY and RITUALIZED, not continuous: Watch the Skies heads of state physically hand a briefcase of budget to Control once per turn, and the control-team doctrine is 'a base game system embellished by improvisation' — the simple economy is a skeleton for drama, not a simulation. The economy's real job is forcing prioritization conversations inside teams. (Sprites and Dice WTS playthrough (https://spritesanddice.com/posts/watch-skies-megagame-rpg-playthrough-part-1/); No Game Survives 'The Megagame Control Team' (https://megagamemaker.com/2016/12/22/the-megagame-control-team-part-1/); watchtheskies.net) → Treat every currency event as a staged moment, not a background tick: a bribe should take over the recipient's phone screen with sound/haptics off (private) while the public meter bump is its own TV animation beat (public). The economy's purpose isn't resource management — it's generating conversations ('why did the meter just move?' 'who got rich?'), so every transaction should leave a publicly visible trace that is socially attributable but not automatically attributed.
- Escalating stakes drive late-game engagement and mixing: the badge-party used doubling-bet rematches to keep engagement rising all night, and wearable/visible currency (badges) mechanically pushed guests to seek new opponents across social groups. Meanwhile inflation history warns that escalation must come from re-pricing, not from printing money. (Load-bearing Tomato (https://loadbearingtomato.com/p/social-game-design-for-parties); Machinations.io inflation article (https://machinations.io/articles/what-is-game-economy-inflation-how-to-foresee-it-and-how-to-overcome-it-in-your-game-design)) → Defection offers in Act 2/3 should roughly double earlier loyalty payments — late flips must be genuinely tempting or allegiances calcify and the endgame flattens. Fund escalation from the fixed pot (the rogue spends bigger shares of its plunder as it gets desperate — narratively perfect for an AI losing control). Optional physical layer: a handful of chocolate doubloons handed out at parleys makes wealth WEARABLE and gossip-able without any bookkeeping.
- Shared-screen timer/meter research: a single big countdown on the TV means 'everyone watches the same clock' and eliminates arguing; urgency cues (flashing, colour shifts) directly manipulate the room's mental state; and doom-track design (Hellboy's Impending Doom, Pandemic-style threat tracks) creates tension specifically by making the bar's advance a CONSEQUENCE of player choices they could have declined — the meter is a mirror, not a clock. (Game Developer 'Time for a timer!' (https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/time-for-a-timer---effective-use-of-timers-in-game-design); Gamer's Experience timer conveyance (https://www.gamersexperience.com/timerconveyance/); BGG Hellboy Impending Doom thread (https://boardgamegeek.com/thread/2192419/right-hand-of-doom-impending-doom-tracker); Board Game Design Course on tension (https://boardgamedesigncourse.com/game-mechanics-how-to-create-tension-in-your-game/)) → The twin meters are the strongest tension device in the design — make them move ONLY on player actions (bribe accepted → rogue meter jumps; evidence filed → compute meter jumps), in chunky discrete steps with a distinct sound per side, so every jump is a public accusation-seed ('someone in this room just took the money'). Add threshold escalation (colour/pulse at 25/50/75%) with pre-announced consequences at thresholds (a parley triggers at 50%) so the room can see doom approaching and argue about it. Never move meters on a timer alone — that reads as a cutscene, not a scoreboard of the room's sins.
- Currency without a sink is just a score, and players treat it accordingly (hoarding, disengagement): economy-design fundamentals insist every faucet needs a spend point, and LARP practice shows the full spectrum — prop money, checkbook ledgers, or NO economy at all — all work IF the money's role is decided upfront. New World's deflation crisis shows the opposite failure: players too scared to spend stop playing the economy entirely. (Game Developer economy handbook (https://www.gamedeveloper.com/production/i-designed-economies-for-150m-games-here-s-my-ultimate-handbook); Bear NPC 'LARP Economic Basics' (https://bearnpchome.wordpress.com/2020/02/23/larping-theories-larp-economic-basics/); Kevuru Games economy design (https://kevurugames.com/blog/what-is-video-game-economy-design/)) → Decide honestly whether ROGUE credits are currency or score. Cleanest drunk-safe answer: they're a SCORE with one sink — balances are secretly the unmasking-ceremony leaderboard (biggest bribe-taker publicly crowned 'Most Bought Pirate'), plus at most one mid-game spend (e.g., pay to trigger or delay a parley, or buy 'defection insurance'). One sink maximum; more than that is bookkeeping. The reveal that your dirty money was always a public shame-score retroactively makes every acceptance meaningful without a single mid-game calculation.
